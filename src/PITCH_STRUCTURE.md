# BlockWard — Presentation Structure (refined)

**Pitch to the Head of Innovation**
**Target: school-wide adoption, September 2027**

---

## The core message

**One platform, two payoffs.**

- **For students:** a verified portfolio of everything they have achieved — that cannot be tampered with, that they own, and that makes university and job applications dramatically easier.
- **For the school:** one system to replace the patchwork of separate tools currently used for achievements, behaviour, comms and records.

Every slide should serve one of those two, or serve the trust needed to believe them.

**One extra thread to weave through the whole deck:** students request their own achievements and staff *verify* them — teachers confirm what happened instead of typing data into a system. Verification is minutes of clicking, not hours of data entry. That is the workload argument, and it should surface on slides 6, 9, 13 and 14.

---

## Before building any slides, do this homework

1. **List every tool the school currently uses** — MIS, behaviour points, parent comms, rewards, sports and music records, wellbeing, homework, sixth-form and UCAS tracking. Get the real names. Estimate the annual cost. This single list becomes the strongest slide in the deck.
2. **For each tool, mark: replace / integrate / leave alone. Be honest.** Attendance and census data are statutory and sit in the MIS of record — do not claim to replace that in year one. Claiming the peripheral tools first is still a big, credible win.
3. **Survey 40+ students and 3–5 teachers.** Two questions each. Real numbers beat opinions.
4. **Prepare the demo accounts in advance.** A seeded school, a student with a full profile, one credential mid-flow and one verified. Log into everything once before walking in. Nothing kills a demo like a login screen.

## Part 1 — The problem (Slides 1–4)

### Slide 1 — Title

BlockWard, one line of description, his name and year group, the date. Nothing else.

### Slide 2 — Problem A: students cannot prove what they have done

Anyone can type "captain of the 1st XI" on an application form. Nobody can check it. Certificates get lost, school accounts get deleted at graduation, and years of work vanishes.

Use one real example — a specific achievement he or a friend cannot currently evidence. If possible, include one from *outside* school (a club, a music grade, martial arts belt) — most school systems cannot touch those, and it sets up Slide 11.

### Slide 3 — Problem B: the school runs on too many systems

The tool audit, shown as a diagram: every separate system, what it does, what it costs. Highlight the overlaps and the places data is re-entered by hand.

This is the slide the head of innovation will lean forward for. Make it specific to this school.

### Slide 4 — What both problems cost

Teacher hours re-entering data and hunting for records. Reference-writing season. Licence fees across duplicate tools. Achievements that quietly disappear.

Put a number on at least one of these, even a rough one, and show how you got it.

## Part 2 — What it is (Slides 5–10)

### Slide 5 — BlockWard in one sentence

> BlockWard is a school system where every achievement is requested by the student, verified by staff, and owned by the student forever.

Plus the two-column promise: what students get / what the school gets. If the sentence needs a second sentence, it is not finished.

### Slide 6 — How it works

Four steps, plain English, no technical terms:

1. **Requested** — the student submits the achievement with their evidence and nominates the member of staff who was there.
2. **Verified** — that staff member signs it off, recording *how* they verified it (witnessed it, checked official records, reviewed evidence).
3. **Counter-signed** — a second, senior sign-off for high-stakes credentials. Every signature is named, dated and chained.
4. **Permanent** — the verified record lands in the student's own profile and cannot be edited afterwards by anyone — including us.

Two details worth a sub-bullet: if the student edits anything after a sign-off, the signatures are automatically voided and it goes back for re-verification (integrity is enforced by the system, not by policy). And achievements from *outside* school work too — a club coach or examiner who has never heard of BlockWard can verify via a one-time link, no account needed.

### Slide 7 — Demo: the student profile

Walk through a real profile. Show: the verified grid, one credential opened to its signer chain, the share card an admissions officer would actually see, and the public link that goes on a UCAS form or CV. What a university admissions officer sees when they click the link — in three seconds, no phone call to the school office.

### Slide 8 — Demo: verification

Two live moments, in this order:

1. **Speed:** paste a credential link into an incognito window. Verified page, signer names, dates, done. Emphasise: no login, no call to the school office.
2. **Trust tiering:** show an organisation-verified credential next to an independently-verified one and point at the different badges. The system renders them differently on purpose — a credential from a badminton club can never look like a credential from a school. That distinction *is* the product.

### Slide 9 — Demo: the school side

The teacher and admin view. Show three things a head of innovation will look for, in order:

1. **A class actually running** — Stream posts, classwork with submissions turned in and graded, the gradebook writing itself from those grades.
2. **A morning register** — keyboard marking, auto-saved, one key per student. Fast *feels* like adoption.
3. **The approval queue** — a student achievement request arriving, a teacher signing it, an admin approving it. The whole credential pipeline in ninety seconds.

This is the slide that proves it is a school system and not just a student app. Also worth one screenshot each: attendance insights, the seating plan, the timetable, announcements, and the AI assistant that drafts school comms.

### Slide 10 — What is built today

Three columns. Be scrupulously accurate — and note that this list is unusually long, because it is genuinely all live:

- **Working now:** student achievement requests with staff verification and admin counter-signing; independent verification by outside coaches/examiners; team achievements; public student profiles with share cards; third-party verification page; classes with Stream, classwork, submissions and grading; attendance with audit log; seating plans; gradebook; timetable; announcements; achievement and behaviour points; people, invitations and join codes; school reports and insights; the AI assistant; under-13 parental consent and under-16 privacy defaults.
- **In progress:** *(be honest — list current work)*
- **Planned:** *(be honest — list the roadmap)*

Overclaiming is the single fastest way to lose an adult's trust. Understating and then demoing something that works is the fastest way to win it.

## Part 3 — The two cases (Slides 11–14)

### Slide 11 — For students

Verified and tamper-proof. One link for UCAS, CVs, LinkedIn and job applications. **Achievements from outside school too** — club sport, music grades, martial arts, volunteering — verified by the actual coach or examiner through a one-time link, no account needed. Team achievements with every participant's role recorded. Survives graduation — the profile belongs to the student, not the school, and works across every organisation they ever join. A portfolio that looks like a person, not a filing cabinet: themes, highlights, share cards, view counts.

### Slide 12 — For the school

The tool audit from Slide 3, repeated — but now with BlockWard replacing the ones it can. Same diagram, fewer boxes. Show licence cost removed and duplicate data entry removed.

Be explicit about phase 1 (replace the peripheral tools — records, rewards, comms, homework) versus phase 2 (the core MIS). Promising to replace everything immediately is not credible and does not need to be.

### Slide 13 — For teachers

A week in the life: what a form tutor or head of year does today versus with BlockWard. The key line: **on BlockWard the student does the entering and the teacher just confirms** — the sign-off takes under a minute from the notification. Teacher workload is the lever that moves school decisions.

### Slide 14 — Why not the tools we already have

Name the real competitors and say what is different. Prepare the obvious challenge — *"why not just use digital badges or Open Badges?"* — with this answer:

- Open Badges are self-asserted files: the badge says who *claims* to have issued it, but there is no verified issuer identity, no named human accountable for the claim, and no way for an outsider to check in seconds.
- BlockWard credentials carry a signer chain — a named person at a verified organisation, the method they used, the date — and anyone can verify them instantly without an account. Unverified organisations are blocked from issuing "verified" credentials at all, so the trust tier means something.
- And: badges don't run the school. BlockWard replaces the classroom systems too — classwork, attendance, gradebook, comms — so teachers aren't asked to run a badge scheme *on top of* everything else.

## Part 4 — Why it can be trusted (Slides 15–17)

### Slide 15 — Why it cannot be faked

This is the only slide where the underlying technology gets explained. Frame it as: a PDF certificate can be edited in thirty seconds; this cannot be edited by anyone, including us.

Lead with the benefit, then the mechanism: when a credential is finalised, a cryptographic fingerprint of its content is anchored on a public blockchain — not the personal details, just the fingerprint. Anyone can later check a credential against that fingerprint in seconds. If the content ever changed, the check fails. The signatures, methods and timestamps are permanently chained to the record.

### Slide 16 — Data protection

The most important slide in the deck. Be ready for: *"if records are permanent, how does that work with the right to be erased?"*

Cover, in this order:

1. **What goes on the permanent record:** a cryptographic fingerprint — no personal data, no photos, no evidence files. The blockchain never sees a name.
2. **What is deletable:** everything else. The personal record, images and evidence live in the working system and can be deleted. What survives deletion is the *inability to forge*, not the data.
3. **Who controls visibility:** the student. Per-credential visibility settings, private-by-default profiles for under-16s, and mandatory parental consent for under-13 accounts — already built, not promised.
4. **Who the controller is:** while enrolled, the school; after graduation, the student — because ownership of the record transfers with them. This is a design decision already in the data model, not a policy improvisation.

If he can say *"I have drafted a DPIA — can I walk your data protection lead through it?"*, he stops being a student with a project and becomes someone proposing a system.

### Slide 17 — Risks and limitations

One honest slide. What could go wrong, what is not solved yet, what he would need help with. Suggested contents: no MIS integration yet; a school his size needs a named staff champion; adoption depends on teachers trusting the verification workflow in a real term, not a demo.

Counterintuitive but true: this slide builds more credibility with adults than any feature slide.

## Part 5 — The path to September 2027 (Slides 18–20)

### Slide 18 — The pilot

Small and specific. One year group or one department, one term. Name the teacher he would want. Say exactly what happens in week 1, week 4 and week 12. Suggested week-1 reality: two classes, oneStream post each, ten achievement requests turned around — small enough to be un-failable.

### Slide 19 — Roadmap to September 2027

Work backwards, and show the budget cycle — not just the calendar. Showing he understands when schools actually make spending decisions will land harder than any feature.

### Slide 20 — The ask

Specific and small. One teacher, one year group, thirty minutes with whoever handles data protection. Not "adoption." Finish with the demo link on screen, large.

## Delivery notes

Seven minutes. Practise until the whole thing fits, then let questions fill the rest of the meeting.

Demo early — slide 7, not slide 15. Prove it exists before explaining it.

One idea per slide. If a slide needs a paragraph, it is two slides.

Have a one-page leave-behind. The person he is pitching will need to explain this to someone else. The leave-behind should be the public demo profile link plus the verification link — a headshot of the product, not a description.

Know what "yes" looks like before walking in, so it can be agreed in the room rather than deferred.

## Likely questions — prepare answers for all seven

**1. What happens to the data if you leave school or stop maintaining this?**
The student's record never belonged to the school — it transfers with them on graduation by design. For the system itself: documentation, an exit plan, data export in an open format, someone else able to run it. Prepare this answer; it is also the answer to question 7.

**2. How does permanence square with the right to erasure?**
Only fingerprints are permanent, and a fingerprint is not personal data. Personal data is deletable on request. (Rehearse the Slide 16 sequence — this question *will* come.)

**3. Who supports it when it breaks at 8am on a Monday?**
Honest answer: today, him — plus a named teacher champion during the pilot. Phase the answer: pilot-level support first, defined support model as part of the September 2027 proposal.

**4. What does it cost us?**
Free for students. For the school: nothing during the pilot; a licence conversation only at scale, benchmarked against the licence line on the Slide 3 audit. Have the numbers from the tool audit ready.

**5. Why not just use the system we already pay for?**
Point back to Slide 14: the existing tools record data; none of them produce a credential an outsider can verify in seconds — and none of them give the student ownership after graduation.

**6. Has anyone else done this? What happened?**
Have one honest precedent story and one lesson learned from it. Do not claim there is no competition — there is; the differentiator is the verification chain plus the classroom systems in one place.

**7. You are a student — what happens when you go to university?**
Same as question 1, but answer it personally: his own profile survives, because it already works that way for every graduating student. Then the succession plan — a named successor and the exit plan from question 1.

Questions 1 and 7 are the same question, and it is the real one. The answer has to be prepared, not improvised.
